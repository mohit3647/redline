## Findings detail

1. **Mandatory arbitration + class-action waivers are the highest-volume, best-documented harm.** 56.2% of private-sector nonunion employees (60M+ people) are bound by forced arbitration; 24.7M of those also can't bring a class action. On the consumer side, 826M+ arbitration agreements were in force as of 2018, covering 60%+ of US e-commerce transactions and a majority of US households — yet actual arbitration filings are minuscule (~411/year across six major consumer-finance markets, 2010-2012) compared to the 350M+ consumers who benefited from class-action settlements in the same window. Source: https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers

2. **Freelancer nonpayment/late-payment is a near-majority experience, not an edge case.** Freelancers Union's survey of 5,000+ independent workers found 71% had trouble collecting payment at least once in their career, with unpaid freelancers losing ~$6,000/year on average (about 13% of income). A separate NY-focused survey (Authors Guild) found 62% of freelance workers reported never being paid for work performed. Sources: https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2015/12/10/costs-nonpayment/ and https://authorsguild.org/news/survey-finds-62-percent-of-ny-freelance-workers-have-lost-wages-due-to-nonpayment/

3. **Auto-renewal/negative-option clauses drive sustained, active FTC enforcement.** Despite a 2025 court vacatur of the "Click-to-Cancel" rule on procedural (not substantive) grounds, the FTC's underlying rulemaking record was described by law firms tracking the docket as resting on "dozens of enforcement actions, tens of thousands of consumer complaints." The FTC has continued bringing subscription-cancellation enforcement actions through 2025-2026 and reopened rulemaking via ANPRM in March 2026. Sources: https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/alerts-practices-ba-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-gets-new-life and https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule

4. **Unilateral-amendment ("we can change the deal on you") clauses drew a direct regulatory strike.** In January 2025 the CFPB proposed Regulation AA changes that would forbid covered financial-product companies from reserving the right to unilaterally change material contract terms, on the theory that such clauses let a company impose changes that "mainly benefit the company" while consumers have no ability to reject them. Source: https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/01/cfpbs-proposed-rule-targets-consumer-fin

5. **Non-compete clauses cover a surprisingly broad swath of the workforce, not just executives.** The FTC estimated 18% of the US workforce (~30 million workers, across job levels from fast food to CEO) was bound by a noncompete clause; only <1% of workers fell into the "senior executive" exemption in the FTC's 2024 rule (later enjoined). Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/fact-sheet-ftcs-proposed-final-noncompete-rule

6. **Security-deposit clauses are the single most complained-about lease term.** The Rental Protection Agency, compiling "thousands of complaints" from tenants across all 50 states, ranks security-deposit disputes as the #1 tenant complaint category nationally (ahead of bed bugs at #2). No raw percentage was published in the source I could access. Source: https://american-apartment-owners-association.org/property-management/latest-news/10-most-common-apartment-complaints/

7. **"Unexpected fees" recur as a CFPB complaint theme across financial products**, called out specifically in payday-loan and credit-card complaints in the CFPB's 2024 Consumer Response Annual Report — though this report's top-line finding is that credit-reporting complaints dominate the database overall (85% of ~all complaints), so fee-clause complaints are a minority theme within a minority category, not the headline finding. Source: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_cr-annual-report_2025-05.pdf

8. **Limitation-of-liability and indemnification clauses are the most consistently *negotiated* clauses in B2B/contractor agreements**, per contract-management-industry sources — described as "one of the most frequently negotiated terms in contracts," with disputes frequently turning on whether an indemnification obligation is or is not subject to a liability cap. This is weaker evidence (industry commentary, not a complaint database or survey) but was consistent across three independent legal/contract-tech sources. Sources: https://www.sirion.ai/library/contract-clauses/limitation-of-liability-vs-indemnification/, https://www.icertis.com/contracting-basics/limitation-of-liability-clause/, https://docket.acc.com/node/4168

## Ranking rationale — confidence

- **High confidence, rank 1-2:** Arbitration/class-action-waiver and freelancer nonpayment are each backed by large-sample hard data (worker/consumer counts in the tens of millions; survey samples in the thousands) from independent, credible sources (EPI-derived compilation via Center for Justice & Democracy; Freelancers Union's own large-N survey plus a corroborating independent NY survey). These are the two clause-harm areas where I found genuine *volume* evidence, not just "this clause exists and is bad" commentary.
- **Medium-high confidence, rank 3-5:** Auto-renewal, unilateral-amendment, and non-compete are all backed by direct action from a federal regulator (FTC or CFPB) — which is strong evidence the harm is real and widespread enough to justify rulemaking — but I either could not pull the FTC's own raw complaint count (it was reported secondhand by law firms) or the CFPB evidence is a regulatory finding rather than a complaint-count. I'm treating regulatory rulemaking as strong-but-indirect evidence of frequency.
- **Medium confidence, rank 6-7:** Security deposits and general fee complaints have real sourcing but come from advocacy-org rankings or a complaint database where the clause type is buried inside a much larger "fees" or "tenant complaints" bucket, not isolated as its own reported statistic.
- **Lowest confidence, rank 8:** Limitation-of-liability/indemnification is included because it recurred consistently across independent legal-industry sources describing it as one of the most negotiated/disputed clause pairs in commercial contracting — but none of the sources are complaint databases or surveys, only industry commentary. It's plausible this belongs higher for the B2B/freelancer segment of Redline's use case specifically, but I don't have the data to justify that.
- The ranking mixes two different kinds of evidence (raw complaint/coverage volume vs. "a regulator judged this important") and should not be read as a single consistent scale — I ordered by a rough blend of (a) breadth of population affected and (b) strength/hardness of the source, with (a) weighted more heavily where both were comparable.
